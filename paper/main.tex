\begin{abstract}
Quantitative structure--property relationship (QSPR) studies based on graph-theoretic topological indices are widely published, but each study usually hand-assembles a small drug set, computes a few indices with ad-hoc scripts, and reports in-sample regression fits. As a result the studies are hard to reproduce and hard to compare. \ml{} is an open-source web platform and Python package that turns a disease concept into a fully specified QSPR study. It resolves the disease to a controlled MeSH heading, assembles an approved-drug set from ChEMBL with an explicit clinical-phase threshold and salt and parent normalisation, and writes a versioned drug manifest. It then computes 102 degree-, reverse-degree-, neighbourhood-degree-sum-, distance- and matching-based topological indices, each traced to its primary source and unit-tested against closed forms, an independent implementation and published values. Finally it evaluates ten regressor families under repeated nested cross-validation against mandatory null, molecular-size and element-composition baselines. Every run exports its inputs, database release and settings so that it can be re-executed exactly. \todo{One sentence on the multi-disease validation result.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation and significance}

Topological indices are graph invariants of the hydrogen-suppressed molecular graph \cite{todeschini2009}. They have been used as structural descriptors since Wiener's path-number study of paraffin boiling points \cite{wiener1947}. Degree-based families, including the Zagreb \cite{gutman1972}, Randi\'c \cite{randic1975}, atom-bond connectivity \cite{estrada1998} and Sombor \cite{gutman2021sombor} indices and their many recent variants, are now frequently correlated with physicochemical properties of drugs used for a particular disease \cite{almulla2026antifungal}. The genre is growing fast: Europe PMC records 16 such drug QSPR papers in 2022, 40 in 2023, 62 in 2024 and 113 in 2025, and the share that also applies machine learning rose from 5 to 59 of these (query in \texttt{tools/genre\_counts.py}). We screened the 60 most recent open-access candidates against fixed eligibility criteria. In all 19 eligible disease- or drug-class-specific studies (median 20 drugs, range 10--50) the modelled ``physicochemical properties'' are values predicted by ACD/Labs (via ChemSpider) or computed by PubChem or ChemDraw, not measurements. Within chemical graph theory, index quality is traditionally judged on isomer sets such as the octanes, where molecular size is held fixed \cite{gutman2013testing,furtula2013structure}; drug QSPR studies drop that control. Most such studies share three weaknesses, long recognised as common QSPR pitfalls \cite{topliss1979chance,golbraikh2002beware,dearden2009,tropsha2010best,cherkasov2014qsar}.

\paragraph{The drug set is under-specified} The ``drugs for disease~X'' are usually listed without saying how they were chosen. Approved, investigational and withdrawn agents are mixed, salts and parents are counted separately, and the list cannot be regenerated. Table~\ref{tab:audit} shows how large the choice is. On ChEMBL release~37, a substring query for \emph{diabetes} returns 580 distinct molecules, and the matched headings include \emph{Diabetes Insipidus}, which is a different disease. Restricting to molecules that reached phase~4 for a matched indication leaves 103, of which only 71 are small molecules. Even these include antihypertensives such as lisinopril and valsartan, sodium chloride and vitamins, several attached to the indication through drug-label text mining. So a phase filter alone does not define a disease-specific drug set. A plain-language query such as \emph{breast cancer} matches no heading at all, because the controlled term is \emph{Breast Neoplasms} (1{,}171 indication records for the exact heading).

\paragraph{Index implementations are unverified} Few papers release code, and implementation errors in less common indices (e.g.\ Hosoya~$Z$, reverse-degree variants) are seldom checked against reference values.

\paragraph{Predictive claims are optimistic} Models are often fitted and scored on the same handful of molecules, with hyperparameters tuned on the evaluation folds. Screening dozens of indices against 10--50 drugs also invites chance correlations \cite{topliss1979chance}, and a high in-sample or leave-one-out $R^2$ does not establish predictive power \cite{golbraikh2002beware,alexander2015beware}. Nor are the models compared with the simplest alternative. That many topological indices are dominated by molecular size has been known for decades \cite{basak1987topological,rucker1999topological}, and degree- and distance-based indices of the hydrogen-suppressed graph are blind to element type and bond order (hexane, 1-pentanol, pentylamine and 1,4-butanediol share every such index), a limitation that valence-weighted indices were introduced to address \cite{kier1976molecular}. Yet the size and composition of the molecules are rarely used as baselines in the current literature, so it is unknown how much predictive information the reported indices add beyond them. Current guidance for comparing machine-learning methods on small molecules asks for repeated cross-validation, paired comparisons and practically meaningful effect sizes \cite{ash2025protocols}, and a recent re-test of a small-set graph-index model on five MoleculeNet benchmarks obtained an average $R^2$ of 0.24 \cite{niane2026graph}.

Existing software covers parts of this workflow (Table~\ref{tab:tools}). General descriptor calculators such as PaDEL \cite{yap2011padel}, alvaDesc \cite{mauri2020alvadesc} and Mordred \cite{moriwaki2018mordred} compute many classical indices, and PyTIE \cite{jeyaraj2025pytie} computes degree-based indices and entropies; ChemGraphX \cite{jacob2026chemgraphx} computes degree-, degree-sum-, hybrid- and distance-based indices and graph entropies from structure files; DOPtools \cite{byadi2025doptools} and QSPRpred \cite{vandenmaagdenberg2024qsprpred} automate descriptor calculation and reproducible model building; and browser tools now offer no-code QSAR with ChEMBL access \cite{masud2026browser}. None of these defines \emph{which} molecules constitute a disease-specific drug set, and none evaluates index-based models against size and composition baselines by default. \ml{} is complementary. It does not compete on the number of indices. Instead it turns disease-specific topological QSPR into a reproducible and statistically defensible experiment. Its index values agree with ChemGraphX on all 41 indices the two tools share (43 test graphs, maximum relative difference $1.3\times10^{-15}$; Supplementary~S1).

ChEMBL itself notes that indication data require controlled MeSH/EFO mapping, handling of drug forms and phase information \cite{hunter2025chembl}. \ml{} addresses the three weaknesses above in a single tool that also works for users without programming experience.

\subsection{Architecture}
\ml{} is a Python package with a command-line interface (\texttt{topoqspr cohort}, \texttt{indices}, \texttt{run}, \texttt{rerun}) and a Python API (\texttt{run\_study}) \todo{Fig.~1: architecture diagram; the v0.x web interface is to be ported onto the v1.0 package}. A study is specified by one JSON configuration and runs in stages, each writing an artefact: (i) disease resolution, (ii) drug-set curation (the manifest), (iii) structure standardisation and index computation, (iv) targets and feature sets, (v) model evaluation, and (vi) reporting. Every run writes a run record containing the git commit and whether the working tree was modified, the \ml{} version, the Python version and the versions of all installed packages, the ChEMBL release, the full configuration and its SHA-256 hash, the SHA-256 hashes of the manifest and of every input and output file, the random seeds, the platform and the environment variables that can change numerical results (hash seed and BLAS/OpenMP thread counts). \texttt{topoqspr rerun} re-executes a study from its run record and, with the cached ChEMBL release, reproduces the result tables byte for byte.

\subsection{Disease resolution and drug-set curation}
The free-text input is matched against the MeSH headings that occur in ChEMBL's \texttt{drug\_indication} table \cite{zdrazil2024chembl,hunter2025chembl}. The user then confirms one or more headings, and their MeSH \cite{lipscomb2000medical} descriptor identifiers rather than the typed string are used in all later steps (EFO \cite{malone2010modeling} identifiers are recorded where ChEMBL provides them). Following established curation practice \cite{fourches2010trust}, the drug set is defined by explicit rules (defaults in brackets; \todo{evidence-source default to be fixed before freezing}):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item indication-specific maximum phase $\geq$ a user-chosen threshold (default: 4, approved);
  \item small molecules only (ChEMBL \texttt{molecule\_type});
  \item mapping to the parent compound through the ChEMBL molecule hierarchy, standardisation with the ChEMBL Structure Pipeline \cite{bento2020open}, and removal of duplicate parents by full standard InChIKey;
  \item exclusion, with a recorded reason, of structures that still contain more than one fragment, a metal or no carbon after standardisation, rather than silently keeping their largest fragment.
\end{enumerate}
Stereoisomers (for example a racemate and its eutomer) are kept as separate drugs but share an identical hydrogen-suppressed graph, so all cross-validation folds are grouped by the InChIKey connectivity block to keep them on the same side of every split \cite{kapoor2023leakage}.
The resulting \emph{drug manifest} is shown to the user before any computation and can be downloaded. It lists the ChEMBL ID, preferred name, phase and the reason each candidate was included or excluded. Ordering is deterministic, so a given release and set of settings always produce the same set.

\subsection{Topological indices}
For a molecular graph $G=(V,E)$ (the hydrogen-suppressed graph of the standardised parent structure) with vertex degrees $d(v)$ and maximum degree $\Delta$, the package implements 102 indices in six families (Table~\ref{tab:indices}; definitions, sources and tests in Supplementary~S1):
degree-based edge sums $\sum_{uv\in E} f(d(u),d(v))$, including the bi- and tri-Zagreb indices and the hybrid geometric-harmonic-Zagreb descriptors \cite{arockiaraj2023hybrid};
reverse-degree indices, which use Kulli's reverse degree $c(v)=\Delta-d(v)+1$ \cite{kulli2018} and its generalisation $M_kR(d)=\Delta-d+k$ for $k\le d$ and $(\Delta-d+k)\bmod\Delta$ for $k>d$ \cite{arockiaraj2023ijqc,kalaam2024}, restricted to $1\le k\le\Delta$ so that the map is a permutation of $\{1,\dots,\Delta\}$;
neighbourhood-degree-sum indices, in which $d(v)$ is replaced by $S(v)=\sum_{u\sim v} d(u)$ \cite{mondal2021};
distance-based indices (Wiener, Balaban~$J$ \cite{balaban1982}, Szeged, Mostar, PI, degree distance and Gutman);
the Hosoya index $Z$ \cite{hosoya1971}, computed exactly by dynamic programming over matchings rather than approximated;
and the degree-based graph entropy.
Indices are verified in five independent ways: closed forms on paths, cycles, stars and complete graphs (e.g.\ $W(P_n)=(n^3-n)/6$, $Z(P_n)=F_{n+1}$, $Z(C_n)=L_n$); brute-force enumeration of matchings on random graphs; the Mordred descriptor calculator \cite{moriwaki2018mordred} and ChemGraphX \cite{jacob2026chemgraphx} for the indices they share, on approved drugs and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons; and values printed in the primary papers \cite{arockiaraj2023ijqc,arockiaraj2023hybrid,godlin2026,arockiaraj2025lung}. The last check also exposed three printed values in one source that contradict its own definitions (Supplementary~S1).
An index that is undefined for a molecule (e.g.\ a division by zero) is returned as missing with a reason, never as zero.

\subsection{Model evaluation}
Each target property is modelled with ten regressor families (ordinary least squares, used only when the training set has more molecules than features; ridge; elastic net; partial least squares; $k$-nearest neighbours; support-vector regression; random forest; extra trees; gradient boosting; and XGBoost \cite{chen2016xgboost}), implemented in scikit-learn \cite{pedregosa2011}, plus a mean-only null model. The evaluation protocol is designed to prevent optimistic bias:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Nested cross-validation.} Both the hyperparameters and the choice of model family are made inside each outer training fold only. The outer $k$ folds (default $k=5$, repeated five times with fixed seeds) give the reported performance, so the score of the ``best'' model is not inflated by selecting it on its own test folds \cite{varma2006,cawley2010,krstajic2014cross}. Per-family scores are reported as well.
  \item \textbf{Scaffold-grouped validation.} As a stricter (though still optimistic \cite{guo2024scaffold}) test of generalisation, outer folds are also grouped by Bemis--Murcko scaffold \cite{bemis1996}; acyclic molecules, which all share the empty scaffold, each form their own group. The change from ordinary to nested to scaffold-grouped estimates quantifies evaluation optimism.
  \item \textbf{Mandatory baselines.} Every result is reported next to a mean-only null model and a \emph{size baseline} fitted on heavy-atom and bond counts. A standard RDKit 2D descriptor set serves as an upper reference. The gain over the size baseline, $\Delta R^2_{\text{size}}=R^2_{\text{TI}}-R^2_{\text{size}}$, is the headline metric, because it isolates information that the indices carry beyond molecular size. It is reported, together with the paired change in mean absolute error \cite{alexander2015beware}, with a confidence interval from the corrected repeated $k$-fold $t$-test \cite{nadeau2003}, with a bootstrap of the whole nested procedure as sensitivity analysis because cross-validation intervals can undercover \cite{bates2023cv}.
  \item \textbf{Y-randomised control.} The whole nested pipeline is re-run on permuted targets \cite{rucker2007yrandomization}; a feature set whose real score does not clearly exceed its permutation distribution is reported as uninformative.
  \item \textbf{Applicability domain.} For every outer test molecule a $k$-nearest-neighbour distance to the outer training set flags predictions outside the applicability domain \cite{netzeva2005current}; coverage is reported with every result. Together with the manifest and the run record this addresses the OECD validation principles \cite{oecd2014guidance} (Supplementary Table~S2).
  \item \textbf{Multiplicity control.} Index--property correlations are reported as effect sizes with confidence intervals. Significance is controlled by the Benjamini--Hochberg false discovery rate across all index--property pairs \cite{benjamini1995}, with Benjamini--Yekutieli \cite{benjamini2001} as a sensitivity analysis.
  \item \textbf{Failures are explicit.} An index that cannot be computed for a molecule is recorded as missing with a reason. It is never imputed as zero.
  \item \textbf{Independent targets only.} Duplicate targets (e.g.\ RDKit and PubChem molecular weight) are collapsed. Targets that are near-deterministic functions of atom counts are labelled as such in the report.
  \item \textbf{Reporting.} A completed machine-learning-in-chemistry reporting checklist \cite{artrith2021best} is provided in Supplementary~S3.
  \item \textbf{Explainability (secondary).} Mean absolute SHAP values \cite{lundberg2017} are computed only for models refitted inside each outer training fold, summarised by index family because indices within a family are highly collinear, and reported with their stability across folds and repeats. Attribution is interpreted only where the index model beats the size baseline.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Additional annotations}
PubChem physicochemical properties \cite{kim2023pubchem}, UniChem cross-references \cite{chambers2013unichem}, and Lipinski \cite{lipinski2001}, Veber \cite{veber2002} and PAINS \cite{baell2010} flags are reported as annotations of the drug set. They are not used as modelling targets.
