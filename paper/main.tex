\begin{abstract}
\todo{Abstract describes the planned v1.0; not yet true of the code.}
Quantitative structure--property relationship (QSPR) studies based on graph-theoretic topological indices are widely published, but each study usually hand-assembles a small drug set, computes a few indices with ad-hoc scripts, and reports in-sample regression fits. As a result the studies are hard to reproduce and hard to compare. \ml{} is an open-source web platform and Python package that turns a disease concept into a fully specified QSPR study. It resolves the disease to a controlled MeSH heading, assembles an approved-drug set from ChEMBL with an explicit clinical-phase threshold and salt and parent normalisation, and writes a versioned drug manifest. It then computes \todo{135} degree-, reverse-degree-, neighbourhood-degree-sum- and distance-based topological indices that have been checked against published reference values. Finally it evaluates \todo{11} regression models under nested cross-validation against mandatory null and molecular-size baselines. Every run exports its inputs, database release and settings so that it can be re-executed exactly. \todo{One sentence on the multi-disease validation result.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation and significance}

Topological indices are graph invariants of the hydrogen-suppressed molecular graph. They have been used as structural descriptors since Wiener's path-number study of paraffin boiling points \cite{wiener1947}. Degree-based families, including the Zagreb \cite{gutman1972}, Randi\'c \cite{randic1975}, atom-bond connectivity \cite{estrada1998} and Sombor \cite{gutman2021sombor} indices and their many recent variants, are now frequently correlated with physicochemical properties of drugs used for a particular disease \cite{almulla2026antifungal}. The genre is growing fast: Europe PMC records 16 such drug QSPR papers in 2022, 40 in 2023, 62 in 2024 and 113 in 2025, and the share that also applies machine learning rose from 5 to 59 of these (query in the supplementary material). We screened the 60 most recent open-access candidates against fixed eligibility criteria. In all 19 eligible disease- or drug-class-specific studies (median 20 drugs, range 10--50) the modelled ``physicochemical properties'' are values predicted by ACD/Labs (via ChemSpider) or computed by PubChem or ChemDraw, not measurements. Most such studies share three weaknesses, long recognised as common QSPR pitfalls \cite{dearden2009}.

\paragraph{The drug set is under-specified} The ``drugs for disease~X'' are usually listed without saying how they were chosen. Approved, investigational and withdrawn agents are mixed, salts and parents are counted separately, and the list cannot be regenerated. Table~\ref{tab:audit} shows how large the choice is. On ChEMBL release~37, a substring query for \emph{diabetes} returns 580 distinct molecules, and the matched headings include \emph{Diabetes Insipidus}, which is a different disease. Restricting to molecules that reached phase~4 for a matched indication leaves 103, of which only 71 are small molecules. Even these include antihypertensives such as lisinopril and valsartan, sodium chloride and vitamins, several attached to the indication through drug-label text mining. So a phase filter alone does not define a disease-specific drug set. A plain-language query such as \emph{breast cancer} matches no heading at all, because the controlled term is \emph{Breast Neoplasms} (1{,}171 indication records for the exact heading).

\paragraph{Index implementations are unverified} Few papers release code, and implementation errors in less common indices (e.g.\ Hosoya~$Z$, reverse-degree variants) are seldom checked against reference values.

\paragraph{Predictive claims are optimistic} Models are often fitted and scored on the same handful of molecules, with hyperparameters tuned on the evaluation folds. They are seldom compared with the simplest alternative: many indices are strongly correlated with molecular size, which by itself predicts size-dependent properties well. Moreover, degree- and distance-based indices of the hydrogen-suppressed graph are blind to element type and bond order (hexane, 1-pentanol, pentylamine and 1,4-butanediol share every such index), so any polarity-related signal they carry must come through correlation with size. Current guidance for comparing machine-learning methods on small molecules asks for repeated cross-validation, paired comparisons and practically meaningful effect sizes \cite{ash2025protocols}.

Existing software covers parts of this workflow (Table~\ref{tab:tools}). General descriptor calculators such as Mordred \cite{moriwaki2018mordred} compute many classical indices; ChemGraphX \cite{jacob2026chemgraphx} computes degree-, degree-sum-, hybrid- and distance-based indices and graph entropies from structure files; DOPtools \cite{byadi2025doptools} and QSPRpred \cite{vandenmaagdenberg2024qsprpred} automate descriptor calculation and reproducible model building; and browser tools now offer no-code QSAR with ChEMBL access \cite{masud2026browser}. None of these defines \emph{which} molecules constitute a disease-specific drug set, and none evaluates index-based models against size and composition baselines by default. \ml{} is complementary. It does not compete on the number of indices. Instead it turns disease-specific topological QSPR into a reproducible and statistically defensible experiment. Its index values are cross-checked against ChemGraphX where the two overlap \todo{M3}.

ChEMBL itself notes that indication data require controlled MeSH/EFO mapping, handling of drug forms and phase information \cite{hunter2025chembl}. \ml{} addresses the three weaknesses above in a single tool that also works for users without programming experience.

\subsection{Architecture}
\ml{} is a Python package with three interfaces: a command-line program, a FastAPI REST service, and a single-page web client served by the same process \todo{Fig.~1: architecture diagram}. A run has five stages, and each stage writes a versioned artefact:
(i) disease resolution, (ii) drug-set curation, (iii) descriptor computation, (iv) model evaluation, and (v) reporting. The run record stores the ChEMBL release, the query parameters, package versions and the random seed. Uploading a run record re-executes the same study.

\subsection{Disease resolution and drug-set curation}
The free-text input is matched against the MeSH headings that occur in ChEMBL's \texttt{drug\_indication} table \cite{zdrazil2024chembl}. The user then confirms one or more headings, and their MeSH/EFO identifiers rather than the typed string are used in all later steps. The drug set is defined by explicit rules: \todo{finalise defaults}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item indication-specific maximum phase $\geq$ a user-chosen threshold (default: 4, approved);
  \item small molecules only (ChEMBL \texttt{molecule\_type});
  \item mapping to the parent compound through the ChEMBL molecule hierarchy, retention of the largest organic fragment, and removal of duplicates by InChIKey connectivity layer;
  \item exclusion of structures for which the hydrogen-suppressed graph is undefined or disconnected after normalisation.
\end{enumerate}
The resulting \emph{drug manifest} is shown to the user before any computation and can be downloaded. It lists the ChEMBL ID, preferred name, phase and the reason each candidate was included or excluded. Ordering is deterministic, so a given release and set of settings always produce the same set.

\subsection{Topological indices}
For a molecular graph $G=(V,E)$ with vertex degrees $d(v)$, the package implements \todo{135} indices in five families (Table~\ref{tab:indices}, full definitions in Supplementary~S1):
degree-based edge sums $\sum_{uv\in E} f(d(u),d(v))$;
reverse-degree variants with $c(v)=\Delta-d(v)+1$ \cite{kulli2018} and the generalised (modified) reverse degree $R_k$ \cite{arockiaraj2023ijqc,kalaam2024};
hybrid geometric-harmonic-Zagreb indices \cite{arockiaraj2023hybrid};
neighbourhood-degree-sum variants, in which $d(v)$ is replaced by $S(v)=\sum_{u\sim v} d(u)$;
distance-based indices (Wiener, Balaban~$J$ \cite{balaban1982}, Szeged, Mostar, PI, Schultz, Gutman); the matching-based Hosoya~$Z$ \cite{hosoya1971};
and graph entropy.
Each index is unit-tested against closed-form values on path, cycle, star and complete graphs (e.g.\ $W(P_n)=(n^3-n)/6$) and against values reported in the literature for reference molecules \cite{todeschini2009}.

\subsection{Model evaluation}
Each target property is modelled with \todo{11} regressors (ordinary least squares, ridge, lasso, elastic net, random forest, extra trees, gradient boosting, XGBoost \cite{chen2016xgboost}, support-vector regression, multilayer perceptron, \todo{11th}) implemented in scikit-learn \cite{pedregosa2011}. The evaluation protocol is designed to prevent optimistic bias:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Nested cross-validation.} Both the hyperparameters and the choice of model family are made inside each outer training fold only. The outer $k$ folds (default $k=5$, repeated \todo{5} times) give the reported performance, so the score of the ``best'' model is not inflated by selecting it on its own test folds \cite{varma2006,cawley2010}. Per-family scores are reported as well.
  \item \textbf{Scaffold-grouped validation.} As a stricter test of generalisation, outer folds are also grouped by Bemis--Murcko scaffold \cite{bemis1996}; acyclic molecules, which all share the empty scaffold, each form their own group. The change from ordinary to nested to scaffold-grouped estimates quantifies evaluation optimism.
  \item \textbf{Mandatory baselines.} Every result is reported next to a mean-only null model and a \emph{size baseline} fitted on heavy-atom and bond counts. A standard RDKit 2D descriptor set serves as an upper reference. The gain over the size baseline, $\Delta R^2_{\text{size}}=R^2_{\text{TI}}-R^2_{\text{size}}$, is the headline metric, because it isolates information that the indices carry beyond molecular size. It is reported with a confidence interval from repeated nested CV from the corrected repeated $k$-fold $t$-test \cite{nadeau2003}, with a bootstrap of the whole nested procedure as sensitivity analysis because cross-validation intervals can undercover \cite{bates2023cv}.
  \item \textbf{Multiplicity control.} Index--property correlations are reported as effect sizes with confidence intervals. Significance is controlled by the Benjamini--Hochberg false discovery rate across all index--property pairs \cite{benjamini1995}, with Benjamini--Yekutieli \cite{benjamini2001} as a sensitivity analysis.
  \item \textbf{Failures are explicit.} An index that cannot be computed for a molecule is recorded as missing with a reason. It is never imputed as zero.
  \item \textbf{Independent targets only.} Duplicate targets (e.g.\ RDKit and PubChem molecular weight) are collapsed. Targets that are near-deterministic functions of atom counts are labelled as such in the report.
  \item \textbf{Explainability.} Mean absolute SHAP values \cite{lundberg2017} are computed for the selected model and summarised by index family, because individual indices within a family are highly collinear.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Additional annotations}
PubChem physicochemical properties \cite{kim2023pubchem}, UniChem cross-references \cite{chambers2013unichem}, and Lipinski \cite{lipinski2001}, Veber \cite{veber2002} and PAINS \cite{baell2010} flags are reported as annotations of the drug set. They are not used as modelling targets.
